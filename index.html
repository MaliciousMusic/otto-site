Can a mind grow from almost nothing?

Otto is an artificial life research project. We look for the roots of emergence: the smallest rules and structures from which something starts to hold together.

Then we climb, one measured step at a time, all the way up to an Otto that speaks and understands by itself.

See the mapSee the results

Today: 2 of 21 objectives measured, each in a small domain. Otto does not speak, learn or remember yet.

This is the real experiment, running in your browser. We check that it lands exactly where 769 of our recorded runs landed.

We don’t program the mind. We look for what lets one start.

Otto begins with very little. No words, no goals, no score to please, no language model inside. Only a few physical ingredients, and rules simple enough to check by hand.

Whatever shows up after that has to be explained: what caused it, what breaks it, and whether it was hiding in our own rules all along.

The answer may be yes, no, or only with something more. All three count as results.

    The rule really does something

    64 worlds, each run twice from the same start: once frozen, once alive. With the rule on, scattered cells gather into fewer groups.

    It does not show self-organisation in general. Majority rules are well known, and this is our calibration.

    Order alone can change the ending

    Take one start and update its cells one by one, in four different random orders. 56 of 64 worlds end in more than one place. Pick a world to see its four endings.

    Four orders only give a lower bound. Nothing here adapts.

    Poke it deep and it heals

    Flip one cell of a settled world. Near a border between groups, the world moves for good. Two cells away or more, it repairs itself exactly, at both sizes we tried.

    A direct consequence of this rule. It is not resilience of something alive.

    Green smiles healed. Blue ones moved for good.

    Cut a loop and it stops

    Two bits that keep each other on. Cut one link between states 8 and 16 and the activity is lost at state 9. Give the role to a stand-in and it survives.

    We wrote the rule, so the contrast was built in. A check of our tools, and no evidence of organisation.

    Rejected

    The day we rejected our own measure

    We built a measure of self-renewal and locked its rules before running it. It caught all 198 look-alike paths. Then 38 of 128 candidate paths changed verdict depending on when and how we looked.

    A fact about Otto should not depend on our glasses. So we dropped it, and the next test stays blocked.

    See the numbers

    How we avoid fooling ourselves

    • Decide first. What we measure and what would make us wrong is locked and committed before the run.
    • Look without touching. Measuring never changes the world, and no score ever feeds back into it.
    • Replay everything. Same seed, same world, byte for byte: 1,090 archived runs replayed exactly.
    • Keep the failures. A negative result closes a branch, and that is progress.
    • Wait before naming. When something new appears: observe, replicate, intervene. A name comes last.

    What would count as a win

    1. Something organises, learns and reshapes itself, and we did not program that ability.
    2. It can pass on what it discovered.
    3. It can ask for the information it lacks.
    4. It takes an active part in its own research.
    5. It builds structures that make it better at research.
    6. It can be copied and scaled without falling apart.

    0 of 6 so far. The next one to work on is the first.
